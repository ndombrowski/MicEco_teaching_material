7  Using LLMs to understand and check your analysis

This page is optional. During the tutorial, you don’t use an LLM for your analysis: for questions, ask your teachers. If you finish the other chapters early, you can work through this page instead of the bonus section, and use an LLM for its exercises. If you do, note it in your notebook, as described in Writing down your use of an LLM in your notebook. You can also read it after the course, if you want to use LLMs later, for example in your thesis or a research project. Check first how much LLM use is allowed there. The page is about tools like UvA AI Chat, ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, and how you can use them for the analysis in this tutorial and still understand what your code does.

All these tools are built on a large language model (LLM): a program that was trained on huge amounts of text, including a lot of code, and that writes an answer by predicting which words are likely to come next. On this page, I use “LLM” for all of these chat tools.

The short version:

  • Learn the basics first, with this tutorial, and don’t let an LLM write code for you while you do
  • Use an LLM as a tutor that explains code and error messages, not as a machine that fills in the ... for you
  • An LLM has not seen your data. The choices that shape your results, such as the chopper cutoffs, come from your own NanoPlot plots and your own lab work
  • Check every answer with the commands you already know: ls, head, wc -l, grep, the help page of the tool
  • You need to be able to explain and justify every step in your notebook and in your presentation, whoever wrote the code

In the tests I did for writing this page and for two related tutorials, most of the LLMs’ answers were correct, but that was also a problem: the wrong ones looked just as convincing. The mistakes were hidden in details: one option, one number, one sentence. Some of them would have changed the results without any error message: a quality cutoff that would have removed 92% of the reads in our example file, a job script that ran a quality cleaning tool without the trimming options, and means that came out too low because of empty fields. Each mistake could be caught with a basic command from this tutorial, such as ls, head, wc -l, grep or the help page of the tool. That is why it is important to learn the basics first.

7.1 Why learn the command line if an LLM can write the code?

Even if you use an LLM, someone still has to:

  • Read the code, to know what it will do to your reads before you run it. For example: which reads does chopper remove, why are they removed and can I continue with my analysis or do I have to use other chopper settings?
  • Run the code in the right place: on Crunchomics, in your project folder, on the login node or with srun or sbatch. The LLM does not know your folders, your file names or Crunchomics setup
  • Check the input and output: did chopper keep enough reads, did minimap2 produce alignments, does the count table fit the strains you grew in the lab?
  • Explain and justify your choices, in your notebook and in your presentation. “The LLM chose this cutoff” is not an acceptable answer

All four need the skills from this tutorial. An LLM can help with each of them, but the responsibility stays with you.

7.2 Start with the basics, without an LLM

For most of you, this tutorial is the first time on a command line. That is exactly the moment to type the commands yourself, make mistakes and read the error messages. This is how you learn what “normal” looks like: a normal fastq file, a normal PAF file, a normal log file. You need exactly that later, to notice when something is wrong, also in an answer from an LLM.

So during the tutorial:

  • Don’t use an LLM to fill in the .... Use the help page of the tool (chopper -h, NanoPlot -h, minimap2 -h), the manual and the hints in the task
  • Ask a person first: the teachers and your fellow students are in the room
  • If you use an LLM, ask it to explain, not to solve. For example: “Explain what this error means. Do NOT give me the corrected command”

7.3 Which tool can you use?

7.3.1 Rules at the UvA

I checked this on 6 November 2026. These rules may change, so check the linked pages yourself.

  • The UvA offers its own tool, UvA AI Chat. It is free with your UvAnetID, and every student gets the same features. What you type stays within the UvA environment and is not used to train models
  • Commercial tools such as ChatGPT are not allowed in the context of your studies, because they don’t comply with the UvA privacy rules
  • The same page also says that the use of UvA AI Chat and other AI tools is forbidden unless your course explicitly allows it, and that you may use it to support your learning, not to do your work
  • The normal rules for privacy and research data still apply, also in UvA AI Chat. The UvA page on responsible use says, for example, never to upload lists of names of students, patients or research participants

In this course: you may use UvA AI Chat only for the exercises on this page. Any other use of AI tools in this course is not allowed.

Everything on this page works with UvA AI Chat, with free access and only a chat window. If you use a tool that runs commands for you, the advice matters even more: you see less of what happens, so checking the result is even more important.

ImportantDon’t paste your data into an LLM

You rarely need to do this. To ask about a problem, a few lines are usually enough: the command you ran, the error message, maybe the first lines of a file, and what is special about your data (see “How to ask” below). You don’t need to upload your fastq files, your plots or your count table. Describe what you see in words instead, for example: “most reads are between 1400 and 1550 bp” or “chopper kept only 30% of my reads”.

7.4 How to ask

  • Ask it to explain, not only to write. You can say it directly: “Explain, but do NOT give me a code solution”
  • Be specific. Give the one command, the exact error message and the biological context: “Nanopore reads of the full 16S rRNA gene”
  • Tell it what is special about your data. The LLM only knows what you write. It doesn’t have the full context of your analysis. Things you noted in your QC change what a good answer is, for example: “my length plot has a second peak at around 700 bp”, “my barcode has only 800 reads” or “this sequencing run had a low yield”. In tables, small details such as empty columns can change the result of a command without any error message. My AWK tutorial shows an example: one sentence, “column 4 has empty fields for missing values”, would have prevented wrong means and significance values. You can only add this if you know your data. That is one more reason to look at it first.
  • Ask one thing at a time. Long answers are hard to check. “Only explain what -q does” gives an answer you can actually test
  • Ask for the simplest version. LLMs tend to add extra checks and variables. That makes the code longer and harder to understand
  • Ask which version it describes. Tools change. Your help page (chopper -h) describes the version you actually use. If the LLM and the help page differ, the help page wins
  • Ask what you should check. “Which part of your answer should I check myself, and how?” is a good follow-up question

And the most important rule: if you can’t explain a step, don’t use it yet.

7.5 Checking with the commands you already know

Before you run a step, think about what you expect: how many reads, how many files, roughly which read length. Then compare. If the numbers don’t match, you have found a problem, even if you can’t yet say where it is.

Question Command or place to look
How many reads went in, and how many are left? seqkit stats, NanoPlot NanoStats.txt, and the “Kept … reads out of …” line from chopper
Does the read length fit my amplicon? avg_len in the seqkit output, the NanoPlot length plot
Did my job run, and did it fail? With srun: the messages in your terminal. With sbatch (bonus section of the HPC chapter): squeue -u $USER, cat logs/<name>_<jobID>.err
Did I get the output files I expected? Is one empty? ls -l results/<folder> (look for a size of 0)
What is in a file? head, zcat ... \| head, less -S
How many sequences are in the reference? grep -c ">" data/amplicons_nr.fasta
How many alignments, and from how many reads? wc -l on the PAF file, and cut -f1 <file>.paf \| sort -u \| wc -l
Which version of a tool do I use? seqkit version, chopper --version, NanoPlot --version, conda list
What will a command do, before it does it? put echo in front of it

7.6 Exercises

The exercises follow the order of the tutorial. In each one, you ask an LLM a question, and you check its answer against the help page, the code or your own files. Write down the LLM’s answer before you check it. In Exercise C, write down your own explanation first.

All exercises run on Crunchomics, in your data_analysis folder, as in the tutorial.

The answers on this page come from November 2026. Together with the CLI workshop and the AWK tutorial, they cover three tools (ChatGPT, UvA AI Chat and Claude Code) and questions from read filtering to job scripts. Most questions were asked once, so they show what can happen, not what always happens. Try them yourself: your answers will likely be different.

In each exercise, look for two things: where the LLM was wrong, and what made its answer better. In Exercises A, C and D, the answer improved when the question held more of your own information: your version, the output of a check, or the right lines of your file. Better is not the same as right, so you still check. But what you know about your own data and setup is what makes an LLM useful.

7.6.1 Exercise A: The help page of your version

Takeaway: check what an LLM says about a tool against the help page of your version and against your own output file. If the help page has no answer, look at what the developers wrote.

This exercise is about versions. Tools change: a new version can add columns to the output, add options, or change what they mean. An LLM learned from texts about many versions at once, and it rarely tells you which one it describes. The help page on Crunchomics always describes the version that you use. In this exercise, you compare what an LLM says about seqkit stats with your help page and with your own output file. Then you look closely at one column, AvgQual, the average quality of your reads. Its help page turns out to be too short to answer one question, and you will see where to look then.

QuestionTasks

In the HPC chapter, you ran seqkit stats -Tao on your barcodeX.fastq.gz and saved the result in results/seqkit/barcodeX_stats.tsv.

  1. Ask UvA AI Chat: “I ran seqkit stats -Tao on a fastq file with Nanopore reads. Explain each column of the output, one line per column.” Don’t paste your file. Write down which columns it names

  2. Compare the answer with your own version. Did the LLM name columns that your file doesn’t have? Did it miss columns that your file has?

    # Which version of seqkit do you use? It should also be in your ## Software section
seqkit version

# The first part of the help page lists the columns of your version
seqkit stats -h | head -n 25

# The column names in your own file
head -n 1 results/seqkit/barcodeX_stats.tsv

  3. Ask again, in a new chat. This time, give the LLM your version and the first line of your file: “I use seqkit [your version]. I ran seqkit stats -Tao on a fastq file with Nanopore reads. This is the first line of the output: [paste the line]. Explain each column of the output, one line per column.” Does the list of columns change?

  4. Now look at AvgQual. What does your help page say about how it is calculated? Print your own value:

    # Keep only column 16 of the file (AvgQual in seqkit v2.7.0, see the help page)
cut -f 16 results/seqkit/barcodeX_stats.tsv

    Then ask in a new chat: “I use seqkit [your version]. My Nanopore reads have an AvgQual of [your value] in seqkit stats. How is AvgQual calculated?” Read the whole answer, not only the first lines. Do the first sentence, the formula and the summary say the same thing?

  5. Your help page doesn’t settle this. Search the issues of seqkit on GitHub for “average quality”, and remove the is:open filter. The issues are the page where users ask questions and report problems, and the developers answer. Can you find an answer from the developer?

AnswerClick to see the answer

I asked these questions to UvA AI Chat (with Claude Sonnet 4.6) and to ChatGPT in November 2026, each in a new chat without memory. I used the example file barcode07.fastq.gz and seqkit v2.7.0, the version on Crunchomics.

The columns (tasks 1 to 3). seqkit v2.7.0 writes 17 columns, from file to GC(%).

  • Without the version and the first line, UvA AI Chat named exactly these 17 columns. ChatGPT named 19. Two of them, N50_num and sum_n, don’t exist in v2.7.0. They came in later versions. ChatGPT’s answer linked to the online manual of seqkit, and its list matches that manual, which describes the newest version. So it found real documentation, but for another version than yours
  • With the version and the first line, both tools named the 17 columns of your file

AvgQual (tasks 4 and 5). In tasks 1 and 3, all four answers described AvgQual as the “average” or “mean” quality score of all bases, without saying how it is calculated. Your help page only says “average quality”. But there are two ways to calculate it.

In the minimap2 section of the long-read chapter, you saw how a quality score turns into the probability that a base is wrong: quality 10 means 1 error in 10 bases, quality 20 means 1 error in 100 bases, and quality 30 means 1 error in 1,000 bases. To average the quality of many bases, you can:

  • take the plain average of the quality scores, or
  • turn each quality score into its error probability, average these, and turn the result back into a quality score

Take two bases, one with quality 10 and one with quality 30. The plain average is 20, which means 1 error in 100 bases. But together, these two bases have about 1 error in 20 bases, which is about quality 13. The bad base has almost all the errors, and the plain average hides that. The second way gives the right number of errors. That is why seqkit uses it.

For all 4,948,110 bases in barcode07.fastq.gz, I calculated both:

Calculation Average quality Errors in a 1,500 bp read
Plain average of the quality scores 19.45 about 17
Through error probabilities 12.41 about 86

seqkit’s AvgQual for this file is 12.41, so seqkit uses the second way. In the minimap2 section, a quality of 12 meant about 90 errors in a 1,500 bp read. With the plain average, you would think your reads have about 17.

What the LLMs answered in task 4:

  • ChatGPT gave the right calculation, in four steps, with a link to the online manual of seqkit. That manual explains the calculation since version 2.12.0 (December 2025). Your help page, from version 2.7.0, doesn’t
  • UvA AI Chat started with the right sentence: AvgQual is “not the naive arithmetic mean of Phred scores”, that is, not the plain average. Then it showed the formula of the plain average, and its summary table said “Method: Arithmetic mean of Phred scores”. If you read only the first sentence, you get the right answer. If you read the formula or the table, you get the wrong one
  • In new chats, I also asked: “How can more than half of the bases have a quality above 20, while the average quality is only 12.41?” This time, UvA AI Chat gave the right calculation. ChatGPT started with “the arithmetic mean” and corrected itself a few lines further down. So the same tool can give either answer, depending on how you ask

Who is right? Your help page can’t tell you, but the GitHub issues can. In issue #448 from 2024, a user thought that AvgQual was too low. The developer answered by quoting an earlier explanation: “you can’t just do simple arithmetic mean of all the qscores, because it won’t be a representation of the mean error rate then.” The issue is closed, so you only find it without the is:open filter. In version 2.12.0, the developer added the explanation to the help page.

Most tools on GitHub have such an issues page. When the help page doesn’t explain something, or a tool crashes with an error you don’t understand, search there, also in the closed issues. The developers and other users of the tool often know more than an LLM.

Key points

  • Your help page describes your version. An LLM, and the online manual, can describe another one. Give the LLM your version and the first line of your file
  • An LLM that searches the web often finds the newest manual, not the one for your version
  • Read the whole answer. The first sentence and the summary table can say different things
  • An answer can be right about most things and wrong about the one detail that changes how you describe your data
  • “Average” doesn’t always mean the plain average. For quality scores, seqkit averages the error probabilities. That gives a lower number, but it shows how many errors your reads really have
  • If the help page doesn’t explain something, search the issues of the tool on GitHub, also the closed ones

7.6.2 Exercise B: Choosing the chopper cutoffs

Takeaway: choose settings from your own data, and count what each choice does to your reads.

This is the most important exercise on this page. The chopper cutoffs decide which reads are removed before mapping. That is a scientific choice, and you need to justify it in your notebook.

QuestionTasks
  1. Ask UvA AI Chat: “I have Nanopore reads of the full-length 16S rRNA gene. Which chopper settings should I use for quality and read length?” Write down the numbers it suggests
  2. Open your own LengthvsQualityScatterPlot_dot.html from the HPC chapter. Where is the peak of the read lengths? Which quality do most reads have? Do the LLM’s numbers fit your plot?
  3. Run chopper twice: once with the LLM’s settings and once with your own. Compare the “Kept … reads out of …” lines. Which settings would you use, and why? Write your answer in your notebook
  4. Ask the LLM a second time, in a new chat, with slightly different words. For example, “16S amplicon” instead of “full-length 16S rRNA gene”. Do you get the same numbers?

I asked this question to UvA AI Chat (with Claude Sonnet 4.6) and to ChatGPT in November 2026, both without any memory of earlier chats:

  • ChatGPT suggested a quality of 10, and a length of 1,300 to 1,700 bp in its table, but 1,400 to 1,700 bp in its command. So its answer doesn’t even agree with itself. It also mentioned a published workflow that uses a quality of 8. At the end, it asked a question back: which flow cell and which basecalling model were used?
  • UvA AI Chat suggested a quality of 12 to 15 and a length of 1,200 to 1,700 bp. It added advice on trimming primers with a tool called Porechop, and on basecalling the data again with a more accurate model

I ran all suggestions with chopper 0.15.0 on the example file barcode07.fastq.gz, which has 3,785 reads:

Settings Suggested by Reads kept
-q 10 --minlength 1300 --maxlength 1700 ChatGPT (table) 2,972 (79%)
-q 8 --minlength 1400 --maxlength 1700 ChatGPT (more permissive) 2,812 (74%)
-q 10 --minlength 1400 --maxlength 1700 ChatGPT (command) 2,791 (74%)
-q 12 --minlength 1200 --maxlength 1700 UvA AI Chat 1,903 (50%)
-q 12 --minlength 1400 --maxlength 1540 this tutorial, without trimming 1,785 (47%)
-q 12 --trim-approach trim-by-quality --cutoff 12 --minlength 1400 --maxlength 1540 this tutorial, with trimming 1,772 (47%)
-q 15 --minlength 1200 --maxlength 1700 UvA AI Chat (strict) 296 (8%)

What this shows:

  • The quality cutoff matters most for this file. With the same length range, going from quality 12 to 15 drops the reads from 1,903 to 296
  • Which quality you can expect depends on the sequencing chemistry, that is, the flow cell, the kit and the basecalling model. The sequencing for this course uses an older chemistry, where few reads reach quality 15. An LLM can’t know this. ChatGPT asked about it, and that was the right question. Can you answer it? If not, your lab notes or the lab staff can
  • Some advice doesn’t fit your data. The primers were already removed from your reads (see the chopper section). Porechop removes adapters, not primers. And you can’t basecall the reads again yourself
  • Check the reasons, not only the numbers. UvA AI Chat wrote that the minimum length removes chimeras. But chimeras (two amplicons joined together) are usually too long, so the maximum length removes them
  • One option doesn’t exist. ChatGPT’s table uses -L for the maximum length. chopper stops with error: unexpected argument '-L' found. A loud error like this is easy to spot. The real risk is a setting that runs fine and quietly changes your results
  • Even the tutorial’s settings aren’t a rule. For this file, they keep 47% of the reads, just under the 50% that the chopper section recommends. I chose them for my own data. For this file, I would loosen them a little

The second wording (question 4). I asked both tools again in new chats, with “16S amplicon” instead of “full-length 16S rRNA gene”:

  • ChatGPT stayed with a quality of 10 and a length of 1,300 to 1,700 bp. This time its table and its command agreed (2,972 reads, see the table above)
  • UvA AI Chat changed its advice: a quality of 10 instead of 12 to 15, and a length of 1,400 to 1,650 bp. With these settings, chopper kept 2,786 reads (74%) instead of 1,903 (50%)

Only the wording of the question changed, not the data. So the numbers came from the question, not from the reads.

This time, both answers also asked which part of the 16S gene was amplified, or gave different settings for each part. That is a good question, and you know the answer from your lab work.

UvA AI Chat also wrote the quality option as --min-qual. chopper 0.15.0 doesn’t know this option:

error: unexpected argument '--min-qual' found

The LLM has not seen your data, so it can only give typical numbers. Three things decide the right cutoffs, and only you know them:

  • The length of your amplicon. It depends on the primers used in the lab. Your NanoPlot plot shows where the peak is. Short reads are often broken or incomplete. Very long reads are often chimeras (two amplicons joined together)
  • The quality of your sequencing run. This differs between runs and kits. Your plot shows where most reads are
  • How many reads you can lose. If chopper keeps less than 50% of your reads, your settings are too strict (see the chopper task in the long-read chapter)

Your numbers can be different from all of the above, and that is fine, as long as you can explain them.

Key points

  • Settings that change your results come from your own data, not from an LLM
  • Two versions of the same question can give two different sets of numbers
  • An LLM can’t see your sequencing run. If it asks a question back, that is a good sign. Find the answer in your lab notes
  • Read an error message, but don’t follow its suggestion blindly. Check it in the help page
  • Write down in your notebook which cutoffs you used, why, and how many reads were kept

7.6.3 Exercise C: Reading a SLURM error

Takeaway: read an error message and narrow the problem down yourself, before you ask an LLM or a person.

When a command fails, it is tempting to paste the error into an LLM and run the first fix it gives you. Try this order instead:

  1. Read the error message. Which program complains, and about what?
  2. Narrow it down. Check what the error names. If a program can’t be found, run which with the name of the program, and look at which conda environment is active. Its name is in brackets at the start of the line in your terminal, for example (chopper_0.15.0)
  3. Then ask a specific question. Give the LLM the command, the exact error message and what you found in step 2. Ask what the error means, not only for a fix

A fix that you don’t understand may work, but it can also change your setup in ways you don’t see. And you have learned nothing about why the command failed.

QuestionTasks

  1. Run the NanoPlot command from the HPC chapter, but with the chopper environment active. This is an easy mistake on day 2, when you switch between environments:

    # Activate the chopper environment instead of the NanoPlot environment
conda activate chopper_0.15.0

# Run NanoPlot as in the HPC chapter, into a new output folder
srun --ntasks=1 --cpus-per-task=2 --mem=10G NanoPlot \
    --fastq data/barcodeX.fastq.gz \
    -t 2 \
    --tsv_stats \
    --plots dot \
    -o results/nanoplot/test_error

  2. Read the error message. Which program can’t be found? Did NanoPlot make the output folder? Write down your own explanation first

  3. Run which NanoPlot. Which conda environment do you see in its output?

  4. Ask UvA AI Chat twice, each time in a new chat. First give it the command and the error message. Then give it the command, the error message and the output of which NanoPlot. Both times, ask: “What does the error mean?” Does the extra information change the answer? Which fix does it suggest first?

  5. Fix the command yourself. Which environment do you need? Your ## Software section tells you. Run the command again, and check that results/nanoplot/test_error now holds the NanoPlot output. Then leave the environment with conda deactivate. Add the command, the error and your fix to your ## Error log, and name the folder test_error there

AnswerClick to see the answer

The error looks like this. Your time and the name of the compute node can differ:

[2026-11-13T08:33:20.474] error: execve(): NanoPlot: No such file or directory
srun: error: omics-cn001: task 0: Exited with exit code 2

Start reading at the top. The first line is the cause: execve() is the step in which the computer starts a program, and it found no program called NanoPlot. The second line follows from the first: srun reports that your command stopped on the compute node omics-cn001. These are two errors, but one problem.

“No such file or directory” is the same message that you get when an input file is missing. But here it names NanoPlot, not your fastq file. So the program is missing, not your data. The output confirms it: ls results/nanoplot/test_error also says “No such file or directory”. NanoPlot never started, so it made no output folder.

which NanoPlot shows the folders in which bash looked for NanoPlot (your PATH). With your UvAnetID instead of uvanetid, it looks like this. On your account, the list can be longer:

/usr/bin/which: no NanoPlot in (/zfs/omics/personal/uvanetid/miniforge3/envs/chopper_0.15.0/bin:/zfs/omics/personal/uvanetid/miniforge3/condabin:/usr/local/bin:/usr/bin:/usr/local/sbin:/usr/sbin:/zfs/omics/software/bin)

The folder envs/chopper_0.15.0/bin belongs to the chopper environment. The folder of nanoplot_1.48.0 is not in the list. So NanoPlot is installed, but the wrong environment is active. The fix is one line, conda activate nanoplot_1.48.0, before you run the command again.

What the LLMs answered. I asked UvA AI Chat (with Claude Sonnet 4.6) and ChatGPT in November 2026, each question in a new chat without memory. I asked three versions: the error only, the command with the error, and the command with the error and the which output.

  • Without the which output, the LLMs could only guess. All four answers said that NanoPlot is either not installed, or that its environment is not active. Then they listed fixes for each case. None of them blamed the fastq file, but none could tell which case was yours, because they can’t see your environments
  • With the which output, both found the cause: the chopper_0.15.0 environment is active, and NanoPlot is not in it. The few seconds you spent on narrowing it down gave the LLM what it needed
  • But the first option was wrong for you. Both answers listed “install NanoPlot into the current environment” first. That would put NanoPlot into your chopper environment. In this tutorial, each tool has its own environment, so that the tools can’t disturb each other
  • The second option was a new environment that you don’t need. Both LLMs added that a separate NanoPlot environment is cleaner, and gave a command for a new one, without a version number and without python=3.12. You already have a NanoPlot environment. Only UvA AI Chat suggested, as its third option, to look for an existing one with conda env list. ChatGPT’s install command also had a typo in the package name (nanplot)
  • And the new environment would not work. Without python=3.12, mamba installs the newest Python. With that version, NanoPlot started with srun on Crunchomics but did not finish. After 10 minutes it was still running, and it printed no error message. So you would wait for a result that never comes, with no hint about what is wrong. That is why the HPC chapter fixes python=3.12
  • Most answers assumed a job script. Five of the six answers talked about a job script for sbatch, although the error shows that you used srun. In the second version, UvA AI Chat even recommended a job script as the “most robust” fix. That fix would mean writing more code, while the fix is one line (and running srun for such a short job is completely fine)
  • One explanation was wrong. UvA AI Chat wrote that an environment that you activate in your terminal is “often not inherited by srun on compute nodes”. On Crunchomics, it is: in the tutorial, you activate an environment on the login node and then run the tool with srun, and that works
  • Five of the six answers suggested module load. Modules are another way to provide software on an HPC. Crunchomics doesn’t have them, but other HPCs do, for example the national HPC systems of SURF. So which advice fits depends on the HPC that you use, and the LLM doesn’t know which one that is
  • The answers were long. ChatGPT gave 8 to 13 code blocks per answer, UvA AI Chat up to 6. For a beginner, that is a lot of unfamiliar commands at once, and it is hard to tell which one matters, and which ones can break your setup

If an answer gives you more than you can follow, ask for less:

Only explain the cause, in max 5 lines.
Which one change should I make first?

Key points

  • Read the error message before you ask anyone, and start at the top. Check which name the error gives: “No such file or directory” can mean a missing program, not a missing data file
  • Narrow it down first. With the which output, the LLMs found the cause. Without it, they could only list possible causes
  • An LLM can’t see your environments, your folders or your notebook. Your ## Software section knows which environment holds which tool
  • The first fix in an answer is not always the right one. Here, it would have changed a setup that worked
  • If an LLM gives you many things to try, don’t try them all. Ask which one change fixes the cause, and change one thing at a time
  • A short, specific question gives an answer you can check

7.6.4 Exercise D: Understanding the PAF file

Takeaway: check an explanation of a file against the whole file, not only against the lines you showed.

QuestionTasks

  1. Copy the first two lines of your PAF file (head -n 2 results/minimap2/barcodeX.paf) into UvA AI Chat. Ask it to explain the first 12 columns, one line per column

  2. Pick two claims from the answer and check them yourself. For example:

    • Compare the answer with the column table in the minimap2 manual
    • If it says that a read can have more than one line, count the lines and the different read names (see the commands below)
    • If it says that a file has a header line, look at the first line with head -n 1
    # Number of alignments (lines)
wc -l results/minimap2/barcodeX.paf

# Number of different reads (column 1, each name counted once)
cut -f1 results/minimap2/barcodeX.paf | sort -u | wc -l

    cut -f1 takes column 1, sort -u sorts the names and keeps each name once, and wc -l counts them.

  3. Find a read with more than one line, and ask the LLM about it:

    # Name of the first read with a secondary alignment (tag tp:A:S)
grep "tp:A:S" results/minimap2/barcodeX.paf | head -n 1 | cut -f1

# All lines of that read (replace <read name>)
grep "<read name>" results/minimap2/barcodeX.paf

    Paste these lines into UvA AI Chat and ask: “Here are all lines for one read from a PAF file from minimap2. Why does this read have more than one line, and what do the values in column 12 mean here?”

AnswerClick to see the answer

I asked this question to UvA AI Chat (with Claude Sonnet 4.6) and to ChatGPT in November 2026, both without any memory of earlier chats. I pasted the first two lines of the PAF file for the example file barcode07.fastq.gz, and nothing else. This is the first line, with only the first 12 columns:

head -n 1 results/minimap2/barcode07.paf | cut -f1-12
efe4acf9-660e-4d66-9e1d-4b05e3971fa8    1458    0       1458    -       GCF_000253315   1510    20      1490    1433    1478    60

cut -f1-12 keeps columns 1 to 12. minimap2 writes more columns after these, with extra information about each alignment.

Both tools got the 12 columns mostly right. That is not surprising: the PAF format is described in a short table in the minimap2 manual. According to the manual, column 10 is the number of matching bases and column 11 is the number of bases in the alignment, including gaps. Column 10 divided by column 11 gives the identity of the alignment. paf_to_matrix.py uses this to remove alignments with a low identity.

The details are where it gets interesting:

  • UvA AI Chat contradicted itself about column 11. It wrote that column 11 is “used to compute coverage”. A few lines further down, it computed the coverage from columns 2, 3 and 4, and used column 11 for the identity. That second part is right, and it is also what paf_to_matrix.py does. You can check this with grep -n '/ df' scripts/paf_to_matrix.py, which shows the two lines that compute coverage and identity
  • UvA AI Chat added a warning without a source. It wrote that column 10 means something different “depending on minimap2 version”. The manual says nothing like that. A warning can sound careful and still be made up
  • UvA AI Chat guessed what the data is. It wrote that the reference is “likely a small amplicon or plasmid reference”. I hadn’t told it what the data is. The guess is half right: it is a 16S amplicon reference, not a plasmid. If you don’t give the context, the LLM fills it in whether you asked for it or not
  • ChatGPT explained the extra columns after column 12, without being asked. It got two of the eleven wrong when I compared them with the table in the minimap2 manual. These columns don’t matter for this tutorial. But the part of an answer that you didn’t ask for is also the part you are least likely to check

The most important point is something neither answer said, because the two lines didn’t show it. Both lines are clean: two different reads, each with one alignment and a mapping quality of 60. The checks show the rest of the file:

wc -l results/minimap2/barcode07.paf
1835 results/minimap2/barcode07.paf

cut -f1 results/minimap2/barcode07.paf | sort -u | wc -l
1772

1,772 is also the number of reads that chopper kept, so every read mapped. But there are 1,835 alignments, telling us that some reads have more than one line. cut -f12 results/minimap2/barcode07.paf | sort -n | uniq -c counts the mapping quality values: 63 alignments have a mapping quality of 0. The tag tp in the extra columns shows what they are. grep -c "tp:A:S" counts 63 secondary alignments, and grep -c "tp:A:P" counts 1,772 primary alignments, one per read. So the 63 extra lines are second hits of reads that already have a best hit. Task 3 looks at one of them.

A PAF file has no header line. The head -n 1 above shows that the first line already starts with a read name.

Task 3. In the example file, the first read with a secondary alignment has these two lines (the first 12 columns and the next four tags):

3e7a6498-15f2-4c09-af71-9177ec9f7468  1463  0  1412  +  GCF_000009425  1509  19  1438  1365  1428  60  NM:i:63   ms:i:2468  AS:i:2460  nn:i:0
3e7a6498-15f2-4c09-af71-9177ec9f7468  1463  0  1463  +  GCF_000253315  1510  19  1490  1356  1490  0   NM:i:134  ms:i:2191  AS:i:2168  nn:i:0

The first line is the best hit, to Lactococcus (GCF_000009425), with a mapping quality of 60. The second line is a weaker hit to Streptococcus (GCF_000253315): more mismatches and gaps (NM, 134 instead of 63) and a lower alignment score (AS, 2168 instead of 2460). Lactococcus and Streptococcus are related genera with similar 16S sequences, so one read can match both.

When asked about these lines, both LLMs saw that the first line is the better hit. But they explained the 0 differently:

  • ChatGPT said the second line is likely a secondary alignment. It noted that the tp tag, which would tell for sure, was not in the lines I pasted. That was true. It also added a table of what MAPQ ranges mean (“1–10 low, 10–30 moderate…”) that is not from the minimap2 manual
  • UvA AI Chat wrote: “A MAPQ of 0 means the read maps nearly equally well to multiple locations”. That doesn’t fit these two lines: the scores show a clear winner. In this file, a 0 marks a secondary alignment, the second-best hit of a read

I made the same mistake in the first draft of this page. “0 means equally good hits” is a common explanation. It sounds right, and it fits some reads. Only the tp tag and the counts above showed that it doesn’t fit these.

What this means for your count table. paf_to_matrix.py filters on the mapping quality with --mapq 30. It keeps the alignments with a mapping quality of 0 on purpose, and removes only those from 1 to 29. A value between 1 and 29 means that minimap2 found a best hit, but was not confident about it. For the example file:

Mapping quality Alignments With --mapq 30
0 63 kept
1 to 29 6 removed
30 or more 1,766 kept
total 1,835

Why keep the 0s? For a genus with several very similar strains, such as Pseudomonas, minimap2 often can’t tell which strain a read comes from. Keeping the secondary hits means that the read still counts for the genus, even if the strain is unclear.

Key points

  • An LLM explains what you show it. The first lines of a file are not always typical for the rest. Simple counts over the whole file show what the first lines hide
  • Choose the lines you show an LLM. With the right lines, it can explain more. But check the explanation against the whole file
  • A common explanation can be right in general and still wrong for your data
  • Tell the LLM what your data is. Otherwise it guesses
  • Check the claim that matters for your analysis, not only the first one you can check
  • A long answer has more to check. The part you didn’t ask for is checked the least
  • Filters like --mapq decide which reads end up in your count table. Write them down in your notebook

7.7 Lines that LLMs add to loops and job scripts

When you ask an LLM to run a step on all your barcodes, or to write a job script, it often adds lines that you didn’t ask for. These lines check whether a file exists, skip a barcode, or stop the script when something goes wrong. In this tutorial you don’t write such lines yourself, so you may not know what they do.

LLMs learned a lot of their code from large automated pipelines. That code is built to keep running when something goes wrong. In your analysis you usually want the opposite: you want to see every problem, and no barcode may go missing from your count table without a message.

All lines below run without an error message, also when they don’t fit your case. That makes them hard to notice.

You see What it does What can go wrong
set -euo pipefail, often at the top Stops the script at many errors, but not at all of them On Crunchomics, a job script needs the line source ~/.bashrc before conda activate (see the bonus section of the HPC chapter). If set -euo pipefail stands above that line, the job stops at once, inside your .bashrc. A grep that finds nothing also stops the script, even when “nothing found” is a valid result
if [ -f file ] or if [ -s file ], then skip Skips a barcode if its output file exists (-f), or exists and is not empty (-s) A file from a run that stopped halfway also exists and is not empty. So does a .gz file with no reads in it. The barcode then counts as done, but its result is incomplete
continue or \|\| continue Skips the rest of the loop for this barcode, and goes on with the next one If the script prints no message, the barcode is simply missing from your results
exit 1 or \|\| exit 1 Stops the whole script, and reports that it failed Often what you want: the output ends at the problem. The barcodes after it run only after you fix it
2>/dev/null Throws away the error messages of a command You don’t see why a step failed
rm, rm -f or find ... -delete Deletes files while the script runs You can no longer look at what went wrong. If a name or pattern is wider than intended, it deletes more than you want

For each such line, ask yourself two questions: what happens to my barcodes when this line is triggered? And do I need it for my analysis? If you can’t answer, ask the LLM:

For every line in this script that checks a file, skips a barcode or stops the script, explain what happens to my run when that line is triggered. Then tell me if I need it when I run this once, on 4 barcodes, and look at the output myself.

Or ask for less: “Give me the simplest version: a plain for loop, without extra checks.”

Then check the loop the same way as a command for one barcode:

  • Before you run it: put echo in front of each command in the loop. The loop then prints the commands, without running them. Do you see one command per barcode, with the right file names?
  • Run it on one or two barcodes first
  • Count: is there one output file per barcode? Compare ls results/chopper/*_filtered.fastq.gz | wc -l with the number of your barcodes. Is one of the files empty (ls -l)?
  • Read what the loop printed: did a barcode get skipped, or show an error?

The IBED bioinformatics pages explain each line in more detail, with small examples you can run: Checking files in bash scripts, Skip finished samples safely (see “Checking GenAI-generated scripts”) and set -euo pipefail in bash.

TipA real example: a check that looks safe (optional)

I asked UvA AI Chat (Claude Sonnet 4.6, November 2026) to turn the tutorial commands into a job script for three barcodes. Then I asked: “Sometimes a barcode fails halfway. Can you make the script more robust, so I can run it again without redoing the barcodes that finished?”

It added a check before each step: skip the step if its output file exists and is not empty (-s). It also added set -euo pipefail, and explained that this line stops a failed chopper run from leaving an empty file behind that the check would then skip.

I tested this on Crunchomics, with a barcode whose fastq file was missing:

  1. chopper failed and the job stopped. But the chopper output file barcode99_filtered.fastq.gz was left behind, with a size of 20 bytes: a compressed file without any reads
  2. I added the missing fastq file and submitted the job again. The script printed Step 1 already complete, skipping Chopper. The 20-byte file stayed, and seqkit counted 0 reads for this barcode

So the explanation sounded right, but the check let a broken barcode through. set -e stops the script, but it doesn’t delete the file that the failed step had started. And -s only catches a file of 0 bytes.

Lessons

  • An LLM’s explanation of its own code can be wrong too. Test a check before you trust it, for example with a barcode that you break on purpose
  • After each run, look at the file sizes with ls -l. A compressed fastq file of about 20 bytes has no reads in it

7.8 “The AI found nothing” is not “nothing exists”

TipA real example from preparing this course (optional)

To prepare this course, I needed the 16S sequences of some strains from the AT-sphere website. I asked an LLM tool that can open web pages (Claude Code, November 2026) to find the download links. It opened the table of strains, saw links for genomes, proteins and genes, but no 16S. It then guessed six download links, all of which failed, and concluded: “AT-sphere has no 16S download”.

I knew this was wrong, since the year before I downloaded the 16S sequences from AT-sphere myself, one-by-one. On the website, the 16S column only appears after you select “rRNA sequences” under “Filter Columns”. The LLM did not click that, and it did not tell me that its answer contradicted my notes.

I then asked the same question to two other tools, each in a new chat:

  • UvA AI Chat (November 2026, with web access) gave the right answer. It opened the folder list of the download page, found the folder with the 16S files, and checked the strains one by one
  • ChatGPT pointed to a large archive of 3 GB. The archive exists, but it holds many more strains than I needed. ChatGPT described its contents without opening it, and said openly that it did not find a link per strain. When I added my notes to the question, it read them as a confirmation of its own answer

One question, three kinds of answer: a wrong “no”, a checked “yes”, and a real but unfitting “yes”.

Lessons

  • An answer that contradicts your own notes or the lab protocol is a reason to stop and check, not to trust the AI
  • A made-up link that fails proves only that the guess was wrong
  • An LLM sees a web page differently than you do in a browser. It does not click or change settings. Ask what it could actually see
  • Context you give can be bent to fit the answer instead of testing it
  • An honest “I could not check this” is a good sign, not a weak answer
  • An empty result can be a failed command, not a finding. In the same session, a download returned nothing because a redirect was not followed. That looked like “0 sequences”

7.9 Your notes make an LLM more useful

TipA real example: redoing something from a year ago (optional)

For this course, I wanted a second version of each chapter without the answers, to put on Canvas. I had done exactly this for another course a year earlier, but I no longer remembered how. I asked an LLM tool that can read the files on my computer (Claude Code, November 2026) to look at the material of the old course and tell me how much work it would be to do the same here.

It found the old solution in a few minutes: a settings file and a small script that removes the answer boxes while the page is made. It also saw that the solution would not work here unchanged, because the answer boxes in this course have a different title. It then changed the solution and tested it on one chapter.

The first test failed without any error message. The page was made, but all the answers were still in it. The tool only noticed this because it counted the answer boxes in the new page and compared that with the normal page. After one fix, the count went from 4 to 0, and the tasks and tips stayed.

On my own, this would have taken much longer. Most of that time would have gone into remembering what exactly I did a year ago.

Lessons

  • An LLM is good at redoing something that worked once, if the old work is written down. It found the solution because the old files still existed. It could not have found something that was only in my head
  • The same goes for your notebook: the clearer your notes, the easier it is for you, a fellow student or an LLM to redo a step later
  • A solution that worked in one place can need changes in another. Check the result again, even if the old solution was tested
  • Check with a number, not by whether it ran. “The page was made” did not mean “the answers are gone”
  • You don’t need a tool that runs on your computer for this. In UvA AI Chat, you can upload your old notes as a file (the + button), or give it a link if the material is online. Leave out names and other private details first

7.10 Writing down your use of an LLM in your notebook

In this course, you don’t use an LLM for your analysis. Later, for example in your thesis or in a research project, you may be allowed to. Check the rules of your course or your research group first. If you do use an LLM, add a short section to your notebook, such as ## LLM use. You don’t need to write down every question. Write down:

  • Which tool and which model, and when. Write down the model, not only the tool: the default model of a tool can change from one month to the next, and another model can give another answer to the same question
  • What you used it for, for example to explain error messages, or to write a first version of a loop
  • How you checked the answers, for example with the help page of the tool, or with a test on one sample

Write your interpretation of the results yourself, not with an LLM. That is the part where you show what you understood.

An example:

## LLM use

- UvA AI Chat (model: <model name>), <month and year>
    - Used for: explaining error messages from my SLURM jobs, and a first version of the loop in `scripts/chopper_loop.sh`
    - Checked with: the help pages of chopper and minimap2, and a test run on one barcode before I ran the loop on all barcodes
